# Imagery & Photography

The default AI website looks like a stock-photo catalog wrapped around pastel 3D blobs. This section is the antidote: how to use real photography with the editorial weight it deserves, when stock is acceptable, how to handle school and event imagery with cultural dignity, and when to throw out the icon-and-illustration language entirely.

---

## 1. PHOTOGRAPHY PRINCIPLES

### What separates EDITORIAL, STOCK, and AI

| Trait | Editorial | Stock | AI-Generated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light | Natural, motivated, sometimes harsh | Soft, even, "safe" | Studio-perfect, plasticky |
| Subject | Imperfect, candid, mid-action | Smiling, posed, eye contact | Tense, symmetrical, uncanny |
| Composition | Off-center, cropped tight, layered | Centered, head-room, isolated | Symmetrical, dead-center |
| Color | Unfiltered, mixed light temps | Warm, lifted shadows | Saturated, hyperreal |
| Grain/noise | Present, intentional | Cleaned to plastic | Airbrushed |
| Specificity | Location-specific, time-specific | Reads as "anywhere" | Reads as "nowhere" |

**The litmus test:** if you can imagine the photo appearing in a regional newspaper, on the cover of a college magazine, or in a physical school yearbook — it's editorial. If it could appear on any corporate "About Us" page for any company — it's stock. If it has no shadow, no grain, no awkward angle, and three identical-looking diverse people smiling at laptops — it's AI.

### Cropping

**Editorial crops (prefer these):**

- **Tight on the moment** — a hand holding a trophy, a child's feet mid-dance-step, eyes looking off-frame. Crop at the jaw, the elbow, the knee. Never at the joint (knees look amputated when cropped at the knee-cap).
- **Asymmetric** — subject on the left third, looking right into negative space that holds a headline. NEVER dead-center with symmetric headroom.
- **Unconventional aspect ratios** — 4:5 (portrait magazine), 3:2 (35mm classic), 2:3 (vertical poster), 21:9 (cinematic banner). Avoid 16:9 and 1:1 as defaults — they're the AI-banner shape.
- **Bleed and break-the-grid** — let one photo per page escape its container, bleed to an edge, or break across two columns.

**Stock crops (avoid):**

- Head-and-shoulders centered with a thumbs-up.
- Wide group with everyone looking at camera, equal headroom.
- Laptop-on-desk-overhead-with-coffee.
- Abstract detail shot of "hands" or "feet walking on a path."

**AI-typical crops (avoid):**

- Hero person looking directly at camera, centered, full body visible, ethereal background.
- Symmetrical "team of diverse young professionals pointing at whiteboard."
- Anything with a perfectly even circular vignette.

### Treatment

Choose ONE treatment per page and stick to it. Don't mix-and-match per photo.

| Treatment | When it works | When it doesn't |
|---|---|---|
| **Full color, ungraded** | Strong documentary photojournalism | Mixed lighting creates a mess |
| **Black & white** | Stage photos with mixed lighting; unifying different time periods | Subjects with strong cultural color (saris, tricolor, Holi) — color is the point |
| **Duotone** (single ink + paper) | Magazine cover moments; unifying 30 amateur photos into one system | Portraits where skin tone matters |
| **Sepia/warm grade** | Heritage/legacy sections, founder story | Modern classroom technology photos |
| **High-contrast B&W with grain** | Sports action, dance performance | Group photos where you'd lose faces in shadow |
| **Color-graded to one palette** | Defining a "year" or "era" of the school | If every photo needs different grading, switch to B&W instead |

**For a 117-photo school site:** pick a single treatment strategy across the site. Recommended default: **full color, light grade** (slight lift in shadows, slight desaturation of greens) for documentary/event photos, with **black & white** reserved for a "Portraits" or "Legacy" section.

### Sequencing and pacing

A page with 30 photos all the same size reads as a catalog, not a story. Pace the photo sizes.

```
PAGE SEQUENCING (the rhythm)

[ Hero photo — full bleed, 1 photo ]          ← 0% scroll
[ Small photo | Headline ]                    ← 10%
[ Headline | Small photo ]                    ← 20%  (alternating breaks rhythm)
[ Full-bleed photo — different feel ]         ← 35%
[ 3-photo grid (small, small, small) ]        ← 50%
[ Tall vertical photo + caption ]             ← 65%
[ Wide horizontal photo + quote overlay ]     ← 80%
[ Group of portraits (4 small) ]              ← 90%
[ Footer ] ← 100%

Ratio: roughly 1 huge → 2 medium → 5 small per page section.
```

**Pacing terminology to use when building:**

- **Anchor shot** — the one big photo per section
- **Breather** — a single small photo with lots of text around it
- **Burst** — 3-6 small photos together (event recap)
- **Panorama** — one very wide image spanning multiple sections

## 2. STOCK vs AI-IMAGE vs REAL — How to choose

### Decision matrix

| Content need | Use |
|---|---|
| Hero of the school's actual campus | **REAL** — even a phone photo with proper treatment beats anything else |
| Group of students in classroom | **REAL** — back row is fine, ask kids to look somewhere off-camera |
| "Our values" / "What we believe" | **NO IMAGE** — typography only, or a single real photo of a quiet moment |
| Annual day stage performance | **REAL** — the actual performance, cropped tightly on the dancer, not the wide empty hall |
| Sports day | **REAL** — mid-action, even slightly blurry beats stock running-shoe photo |
| Independence Day / Republic Day | **REAL** — flag ceremony, unfurling, salute moment |
| Lab / library / computer room | **REAL** — even if cluttered. A real desk with real books beats a stock "modern library" |
| Abstract "innovation" / "future" sections | **NO IMAGE OR REAL** — never stock or AI. Use a real student working on something specific. |
| Founder / Principal portrait | **REAL** — but commissioned or curated, not a candid from a group photo |
| Generic "education" filler | **NO IMAGE** — let the type and whitespace do the work |
| Decorative section dividers | **NO IMAGE** — typographic rule, color block, or one real detail (a hand, a stitch pattern) |
| Background "texture" or "mood" | **NO IMAGE** — solid color, paper texture, or a blurred real photo at 8% opacity |

### When stock IS appropriate

- **Never, for an institutional site with 117 real photos.** Stock is a confession that you don't have the real thing. Don't confess.

### When AI-images are appropriate

- **Almost never.** The Indian school context is the worst possible place for AI imagery — students are real, faces are real, the school is real. AI imagery signals "this is a template someone bought" louder than any other design choice.
- **Acceptable exception:** a stylized, clearly-stylized, non-photorealistic element that is obviously illustration.

### The 117-photo audit checklist

Before building, classify the 117 photos into:

1. **Anchors** (10–15): the strongest, highest-resolution, most evocative shots. These get hero treatments.
2. **Story** (40–50): event recaps, classroom moments, sports. These get grid/burst layouts.
3. **Portraits** (15–20): individual or small-group. Get their own section, tight crops, names + grade.
4. **Atmosphere** (15–20): backgrounds, detail shots, candid throwaways. Use as accents, never as primary.
5. **Discard** (10–15): blurry, badly-lit, unflattering, or duplicative. Don't feel obligated to use all 117.

The discard pile is a feature, not a bug. A museum doesn't hang every painting in storage.

### Full-bleed sections

Used sparingly — once or twice per long page. The photo IS the section.

```
+------------------------------------------+
|                                          |
|        [Photo bleeding all sides]        |
|        Caption overlaid, bottom-left     |
|        in 14px italic, 60% opacity       |
|                                          |
+------------------------------------------+

Rule: full-bleed sections MUST have a quiet moment before and after.
Don't stack two full-bleeds in a row. The eye needs to land.
```

### Image grids with intent (not decoration)

```
THE WRONG WAY (decorative grid):
+----+----+----+
| A  | B  | C  |    All same size, all same
+----+----+----+    framing, all same treatment.
| D  | E  | F  |    Reads as filler.
+----+----+----+

THE RIGHT WAY (story grid):
+--------+--+----+
|        |  |    |
|   A    |B | C  |    A is the anchor (event recap key moment).
|        |  |    |    B and C are supporting details.
|        +--+----+    Different crops tell a story, not a mood board.
+--------+--+----+
| D  | E (caption) |    D is a contrasting moment (candid).
|    |             |    E has caption — the "explainer" image.
+----+-------------+
```

## 4. SPECIFIC GUIDANCE — SCHOOL & EVENT PHOTOGRAPHY

### Annual function / cultural programs

**What works:**

- Tight crops on hands, feet, fabric mid-movement. The classical dance mudra, the spinning ghagra, the moment the spotlight catches gold thread.
- Backstage / preparation: a student pinning a costume, fixing a bindi, adjusting a dupatta. Often more evocative than the staged shot.
- The audience reaction: parents' faces, grandparents clapping, younger siblings watching in awe.
- The curtain-call group, but cropped asymmetrically — three students laughing on the left, the rest cropped out at the right edge.

**What to avoid:**

- Wide shots of the full stage from far away (everyone looks like ants, faces unrecognizable, context lost).
- The "everyone in costume lined up smiling" shot — the standard school-program photo that looks identical at every school in India.
- Stock footage of "Indian classical dance" with a generic pose.

**Treatment:** color, sometimes B&W for dramatic lighting. NEVER desaturate to the point where the costume colors are lost — the lehenga's red, the dupatta's gold, the peacock-blue of a Bharatanatyam costume are the point.

```
GOOD CROP:                          BAD CROP:
+----------------------+            +----------------------+
|   [Hands in mudra,   |            |  [Wide shot: full    |
|    costume sleeve    |            |   stage, dancers     |
|    visible, gold     |            |   small, empty       |
|    thread catching   |            |   seats visible]     |
|    the light]        |            |                      |
+----------------------+            +----------------------+
```

### Group photos (large, candid)

**Problem:** school group photos are notoriously hard — uneven lighting, taller students in back rows, someone blinking, the kid in front making a face. Stock-style "everyone smiling equally" is fake.

**Solution:**

- Don't fight the candidness. Lean into it. A group photo with everyone genuinely laughing at a teacher's joke is worth more than a posed shot with 47 forced smiles.
- **Crop aggressively.** You don't need to show the whole group. A tight crop on the front row, a wide crop on just the middle three, a vertical crop on the side — three different crops of the same photo can serve three different page sections.
- If you must show "the whole group," use a wide crop and acknowledge the imperfection in the caption ("Class 9-B, last day before exams, 2024"). Specificity is dignity.

```
Three crops of one group photo:

+------------+----------+---------+----------+
|  TIGHT     |  WIDE    | VERTICAL|  OBLIQUE |
| 4 faces    |  full    | side    |  angled  |
|  laughing  |  group   | group   |  group   |
+------------+----------+---------+----------+
   Use as:    Use as:    Use as:    Use as:
 portrait   event      sidebar    section
 section    recap      accent     divider
```

### Portrait photography

**For students:**

- Outdoor light > fluorescent indoor light. Always.
- Subjects don't need to smile at camera. Looking off-frame, mid-laugh, or holding a prop (a book, a cricket bat, a paintbrush) is more evocative.
- Head-and-shoulders with environmental background (in the library, in the lab, at the sports ground) > head-and-shoulders against a wall.

**For teachers/staff:**

- Same principles, but posed shots are more acceptable in institutional context. Just don't make it a passport-photo crop.
- A teacher at a blackboard mid-lesson, chalk dust visible, is more human than a boardroom-style portrait.

**The dignity rule:** never crop a portrait in a way that looks like a mugshot. Include some environmental context. Leave headroom — not the awkward 60% stock headroom, but enough that the subject isn't crowded.

### Action / candid shots

**The advantage of action photography:** it can't look stock. Real motion blur, real expressions, real dust. Use these aggressively.

**Technical note:** if your photo set includes any motion-blur or partially-blurry shots, KEEP THEM. They're more valuable than 100 sharp posed shots. The slight blur reads as "this was a real moment" in a way nothing else does.

**Cropping action shots:**

- Crop AT the motion. If a student is running right, crop them on the left third with negative space to the right.
- If they're jumping, leave more space above than below.
- If they're dancing, follow the line of the limb — crop just past the fingertips or toes.

### How to handle varying quality and resolution

The photos will not be uniform. Some are DSLR-grade; some are phone snaps from a parent. The skill: use them as a system, not as equals.

**Treatment strategies:**

1. **B&W them all** — instantly equalizes quality. B&W is forgiving of bad white balance, mixed lighting, phone-sensor noise.
2. **Color-grade them all to one palette** — warm amber or muted earth tones. Even phone photos look intentional in a curated palette.
3. **Assign by quality:** the best 15–20 photos get large crops and prime placement; the mediocre 50 get small crops in dense grids; the truly weak get cropped to detail shots (just the hands, just the fabric, just the back of someone's head) where the resolution doesn't matter.
4. **NEVER upscale.** If a photo is 800px wide, use it at 400px max with `srcset` for retina. Pixelation is worse than small.

**The "image as texture" move:** a low-res photo, scaled up to fill a section with CSS `filter: blur(8px); opacity: 0.3;`, becomes a background. The blur hides the resolution issues. Use this for section dividers, never for portraits or hero shots.

### Cultural context — presenting Indian attire and performances with dignity

This is the section the AI fails hardest at. AI defaults to "exotic" — it frames Indian cultural elements as colorful spectacle, photographed like a travel magazine. The school site must do the opposite: present these as NORMAL, belonging, everyday.

**Principles:**

1. **Context over spectacle.** A student in a Kashmiri phiran walking to class is the same as a student in a shirt-and-tie walking to class. Don't spotlight traditional attire as "the cultural display" — show it as everyday life.
2. **No "look at the colorful saris" framing.** Caption: "Students at the Diwali assembly." NOT: "Girls in beautiful traditional dress celebrating the festival of lights!"
3. **Mix the frame.** If a photo is of a Bharatanatyam performance, the adjacent photo should be a classroom, a sports field, a computer lab. Show the school as a whole, not a "cultural showcase" silo.
4. **Don't over-zoom on fabric.** The crop should be on the FACE and the EXPRESSION, not the textile. AI loves to zoom on patterns. People, not patterns.
5. **Names matter.** "Ananya, Class 11, performs Alarippu." Specific person, specific performance, specific grade. Not "a student performs classical dance."
6. **Hindi/regional language captions are welcome.** "वार्षिक उत्सव 2024" with English subtitle. Don't strip the language for international readability.
7. **No "exotic backdrop."** The dance performance should not be photographed against a busy decorative backdrop if the original photo allows for tighter cropping. Reduce visual noise.
8. **Avoid AI-default poses.** AI will draw classical Indian dancers in the "tribhanga" pose with both hands above the head — this is one of the two or three poses it knows. Real performance is messier, more interesting.

### Privacy for minors

Non-negotiable rules:

1. **Never publish a photo with a real name tag overlay.** "Rohit Kumar, Class 8-A" stamped on a portrait. Don't.
2. **For portraits, get parental consent in writing.** One signed consent per student at admission covers internal newsletters; a separate consent is needed for public website publication.
3. **For event/group photos**, an "opt-out" model is legally weaker than "opt-in." Prefer opt-in consent for any photo where a student is identifiable.
4. **If consent is unclear, use one of these fallbacks:**
   - Crop tight on the action, hands, or back of head — face not visible.
   - Black-bar the eyes (looks like a stock crime-show photo — avoid).
   - Use a wider crop where individuals are not the focus (the whole stage, the whole assembly).
   - Use a more distant shot where faces are below 32px — small enough to not be identifiable.
5. **No full names in URLs or alt text** if names aren't shown. `alt="students performing at annual day"` not `alt="Ananya Patel dancing"`.
6. **Watermarking** is acceptable but should be subtle — bottom-right corner, 10% opacity, the school logo. Not diagonal across the whole photo.
7. **Right to be forgotten.** Build with the assumption that any photo may need to be removed next year. Use a CMS where one image deletion removes it from all pages, alt text, and structured data.

**Before publishing, run a one-page audit:**

- For each photo, can you identify a student by face? If yes — is there consent? If no — crop or replace.
- For each portrait, is the name shown? If yes — is there consent? If no — remove name overlay.

## 5. ICONOGRAPHY

### The problem with Lucide/Heroicons defaults

The AI default: Lucide or Heroicons at `stroke-width=2`, perfectly consistent across the site. The result: every site that came out of an AI looks identical, and the icons are weightless — they don't carry meaning, they decorate.

**When the Lucide default is acceptable:**

- A navigation menu with 5 utility icons (search, user, cart, phone, mail) — these are infrastructure, not meaning.
- A footer with 5 social icons.
- Form field affordances (calendar picker, dropdown arrow).

**When the Lucide default is NOT acceptable:**

- Section headers ("Our Values" with a star icon, "Academics" with a book icon).
- Feature lists ("Small class sizes" with a people icon, "Modern labs" with a flask icon).
- Anywhere the icon is supposed to MEAN something, not just decorate.

### Better approaches, ranked

**1. NO ICON (best for institutional sites)**

```
GOOD:
  "Small class sizes"
    Average 24 students per class

    "Modern science labs"
    Three dedicated labs for Physics, Chemistry, Biology

BAD:
    [👥 icon] Small class sizes
    Average 24 students per class

    [🧪 icon] Modern science labs
    Three dedicated labs for Physics, Chemistry, Biology
```

The icon is redundant. The text says it. A bold number ("24") or a word ("SMALL") is more typographic. The school site has 117 photos — let those carry the visual interest, not a wall of generic icons.

**2. CUSTOM ICONOGRAPHY — a real visual language**

If you must have icons, design a SET, not a download:

- Pick one weight (stroke 1.5 OR fill OR duotone — pick one, not all three)
- Pick one style (geometric OR organic OR hand-drawn — pick one)
- Pick one source (custom SVG set, OR a single illustrator's work, OR your own type)
- Limit to 8-12 icons total. You don't need 40. Most pages use 3-4.

**3. TYPOGRAPHIC ICONS — letters and numbers as glyphs**

```
  [24] Average class size
  [38] Years established
  [1:12] Teacher-student ratio
```

A big number in a contrasting type weight is more honest than an icon. Schools have statistics — show them as statistics.

**4. PHOTOGRAPHIC ICONS — use real photos instead of illustrations**

```
DON'T: [✏️ pencil icon]  Academics
DO:     [Photo of student writing in notebook, tight crop]  Academics
```

The school has photos. Use them.

### Line vs filled vs duotone vs hand-drawn

| Style | Use when | Avoid when |
|---|---|---|
| **Line (1px stroke)** | Quiet, secondary navigation; technical diagrams | Hero sections, feature highlights |
| **Line (3-4px stroke)** | Feature blocks, section markers | Inline-with-body (too heavy for inline) |
| **Filled** | Buttons, badges, status indicators | Lots of small icons — fills get muddy at small sizes |
| **Duotone** | Brand-defining moments, a 4-icon set per site | Body text, navigation |
| **Hand-drawn** | Children's content, primary school sections | Higher-secondary or professional contexts |

## 6. ILLUSTRATION

### When to use illustration

| Use illustration when | Don't when |
|---|---|
| Explaining an abstract concept (how photosynthesis works) | You have a photo that explains it better |
| The site is for primary-school-age children | The site is for parents/teachers/adults |
| You're diagramming a process, timeline, or system | You're decorating a section header |
| The illustration is functional, not decorative | The illustration is just "making the page less empty" |
| The illustration is by a named illustrator with a recognizable style | The illustration came from an AI image generator |
| The illustration is for a printed material (where photos can't easily be used) | The illustration is competing with real photos for attention |

### What editorial illustration looks like

Editorial illustration = New Yorker, Atlantic, NYT Opinion, Indian publications like Caravan or Fountain Ink.

Traits:

- **Specific.** The illustration is OF something, not "an abstract concept."
- **Limited palette.** Often 2-4 colors, not a rainbow.
- **Hand-drawn or screen-printed feel.** Not vector-perfect, not 3D-rendered, not pastel.
- **Asks the viewer to think.** Not "isn't this nice?" but "what does this mean?"

```
AI DEFAULT ILLUSTRATION (AVOID):
- Pastel pink/blue gradient blobs
- 3D-rendered abstract shapes
- Floating geometric figures in soft light
- Generic diverse characters with no specific identity
- "Innovation" / "Future" / "Growth" abstractions

EDITORIAL ILLUSTRATION (PREFER):
- Line art with a single accent color
- Hand-drawn characters in specific situations
- Diagrams with personality (arrows that wiggle, labels in handwriting)
- Cultural specificity (an actual school uniform, an actual cricket bat, an actual Marathi script)
- A clear author/illustrator whose style is recognizable
```

### For institutional/cultural context

A school website is institutional. Illustrations should be FUNCTIONAL — diagrams, maps, infographics — not decorative. If you have a flowchart of the admissions process, an illustration helps. If you have a "Welcome to our school" header, no illustration helps.

**The hard rule for an Indian school site with 117 real photos:**

- If you use illustration, use it for INFORMATION (diagrams, maps, infographics).
- If you want decoration, use a REAL PHOTO cropped to a detail (hands holding a book, a teacher's chalk writing "Welcome", a stitched school crest, a row of shoes outside the classroom).

### What NEVER to use

- AI-generated 3D illustrations of diverse students with abstract shapes
- Pastel gradient backgrounds with floating geometric figures
- Cartoon mascots that have nothing to do with the school's identity
- Generic Indian-motif borders (paisley, mandala, lotus) used as decoration — they read as costume, not as design
- Stock illustrations of "children learning" with the same Western-schoolbook aesthetic

## Quick checklist before publishing any photo-led page

1. ☐ Is this a real photo (not stock, not AI)?
2. ☐ Is the crop editorial (off-center, tight, intentional)?
3. ☐ Is the treatment consistent with the rest of the page?
4. ☐ Does it advance the story, or just fill space?
5. ☐ Is consent cleared (for any identifiable minor)?
6. ☐ Are names not stamped on faces?
7. ☐ Is the photo not upscaled beyond its native resolution?
8. ☐ Does the layout use asymmetric grids, not symmetric stock grids?
9. ☐ Is there ONE anchor photo per section, not five equal ones?
10. ☐ Does the photo present the subjects with dignity (not spectacle, not kitsch)?
11. ☐ If it's traditional attire or performance, is the caption about the person, not the costume?
12. ☐ Would this crop make the school magazine editor nod, or wince?
13. ☐ Are there icons that say nothing being removed in favor of typography or photos?
14. ☐ Are there illustrations that say nothing being replaced with diagrams or photos?
